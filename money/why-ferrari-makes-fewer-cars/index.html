Why Does Ferrari Make Fewer Cars on Purpose?

Most carmakers chase volume. Ferrari does close to the opposite, and its results show why.

Down in cars, up in revenue

Ferrari shipped 13,640 cars in 2025, versus 13,752 in 2024. That is 112 fewer.

Yet net revenues rose 7%, from €6.68 billion to €7.15 billion. Selling slightly fewer cars brought in more money.

Ferrari describes its deliveries as deliberately designed to be substantially flat. The company is not missing its target. Staying flat is the target, and growth is meant to come from somewhere else.

Mix and personalization

The company credited a richer product and country mix, along with personalizations. Buyers pay extra to specify their own car, so the average price rises without a bigger factory.

Divide 2025 revenue by cars delivered and you get about €524,000 each. That figure is rough, because the revenue includes everything the company sells.

Profit per car

Net profit was €1.6 billion in 2025. Divided by 13,640 cars, that is about €117,000 per car delivered, again counting all profit.

The EBIT margin was 29.5%, which is around 30 cents of operating profit on each euro of sales. Compare that with the thin margins at a mass-market retailer like Costco, near 3.9%.

Why a waiting list helps

Ferrari says its order book extends toward the end of 2027 and calls the volume strategy carefully managed. A queue of buyers means no need for discounts. When people wait for the product, the seller sets the terms, and the factory never has to push cars out the door to hit a quota.

Scarcity is a choice, and it only works when demand stays above supply. Hold back too little and the waiting list shrinks. Hold back too much and buyers go elsewhere. Ford, by comparison, posted an $8.2 billion net loss in 2025, though its credit arm earned $2.6 billion before taxes. Different model, different game.
